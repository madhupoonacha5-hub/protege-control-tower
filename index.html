      What this demonstrates against the posting
        

      • Intake, prioritization, resourcing: one queue, a visible score, a work-in-progress limit and a fast lane for urgent, high-value work.
        • 

      • Delivery from concept to launch: privacy review moves to the front and quality criteria are set at intake, so rework falls.
        • 

      • Bottlenecks: stalled work stacks up in a lane you can see, instead of in someone's head.
        • 

      • Operating rhythm and dashboards: six metrics that matter, refreshed daily, plus a weekly brief.
        • 

      • Executive communication: the brief and decision log are generated from the same data, so the CEO never has to chase.
        • 

      • Operator in residence: the injected shocks are the judgment calls I would make live, then automate.
      

      Assumptions and what is simulated
        

      • Team capacity is 6 person-days a day, plus 1.5 a day of privacy review. A researcher out removes 1.5 for five days.
        • 

      • Ad hoc work spreads capacity across every open item. Coordination overhead starts at 25% and rises with open items, up to 55%. The privacy check comes last, so a PII finding sends prepared work back. Quality checks fail 20% of the time.
        • 

      • The operating system keeps overhead at 10% through automation, limits work in progress to 7, scores requests, runs the privacy gate before prep and fails quality checks 7% of the time because acceptance criteria are set at intake. Urgency of 9 or more with value of 6 or more uses the fast lane.
        • 

      • Requests are random but seeded, so both sides see the same stream. All numbers are illustrative, not measured.
        • 

      • Automation hours assume 0.4 hours per request for intake and routing, 3 hours a week for status and the brief, and 0.5 hours per delivery for updates. The privacy gate and CEO decisions stay human.
